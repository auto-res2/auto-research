
# Subtractive Transformer Enhanced Mixture: A Robust and Adaptive Framework for Quantifying AI-Assisted Text Modifications

## Abstract
In recent years, the rapid growth of large language models (LLMs) has spurred considerable interest in detecting and quantifying AI-assisted modifications in text corpora—a challenge that is both timely and critical given the increasing influence of AI on scholarly communication. Building upon prior approaches that employ maximum likelihood estimation with token-level statistics, we introduce the Subtractive Transformer Enhanced Mixture (STEM) method, which replaces simplistic token-frequency features with deep, learned semantic embeddings extracted via a pre-trained transformer encoder tuned on domain-specific texts; this encoder maps input documents into high-dimensional representations that capture subtle nuances in style, tone, and context. The core innovation of STEM lies in its subtractive mixture modeling, where the observed text distribution is represented as the square of a potentially subtractive mixture, formally expressed as \( f(x) = \left[(1-\alpha)\,g_{h}(x)-\alpha\,g_{ai}(x)\right]^2 \), with \( g_{h}(x) \) and \( g_{ai}(x) \) denoting neural networks that map these embeddings to scalar “style scores” (which may assume both positive and negative values) and the squaring ensuring non-negativity while effectively canceling shared stylistic attributes common to both human- and AI-authored texts. This mechanism dismisses trivial modifications such as minor edits or spell corrections and emphasizes the more subtle yet substantial shifts induced by AI assistance. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: \begin{itemize} \item \textbf{Deep Semantic Embeddings:} We replace simplistic token-frequency statistics with transformer-based embeddings that capture nuanced, domain-specific linguistic features. \item \textbf{Subtractive Mixture Modeling:} We introduce a novel subtractive mechanism that utilizes a squared mixture framework to emphasize negative interactions between human and AI stylistic signals, thereby improving sensitivity to non-trivial modifications. \item \textbf{Auxiliary Metadata Regularization:} We integrate an auxiliary loss based on contextual metadata (e.g., submission timing and reviewer confidence) that enhances the adaptability and accuracy of AI-content detection under diverse real-world conditions. \end{itemize} To validate the proposed approach, we conducted a series of meticulously designed experiments. In the first controlled synthetic dataset experiment, each document was constructed as a known mixture of human-written and AI-assisted texts by simulating stylistic modifications through deterministic alterations and AI-generated changes; by varying the ground truth mixture weight \( \alpha \), STEM consistently achieved lower mean squared error in estimating \( \alpha \) and higher document-level classification accuracy compared to a baseline additive mixture model. In a subsequent ablation study, three variants were evaluated—a pure additive model without metadata, an additive model augmented with a metadata regularizer, and the full STEM model combining subtractive mixture modeling with metadata alignment; training procedures implemented in PyTorch (with visualizations via TensorBoard and matplotlib) revealed that the full STEM model converged faster and outperformed its additive counterparts in both individual document detection and corpus-level \( \alpha \) estimation. Finally, experiments addressing robustness under domain shifts were performed by training the model on one corpus (e.g., news articles) and testing it on a substantially different domain (e.g., academic papers) using cross-validation strategies and domain adaptation metrics; despite challenges induced by stylistic differences across domains, STEM’s deep representations and subtractive architecture enabled effective generalization, with only marginal performance degradation relative to traditional methods. Overall, our comprehensive experimental results on synthetic, metadata-augmented, and domain-shifted datasets demonstrate that the STEM method is a robust, adaptive, and nuanced tool capable of providing both corpus-level estimates of AI influence and fine-grained document-level assessments, thereby paving the way for future interdisciplinary work that extends this framework to additional domains while addressing emerging ethical and privacy concerns in data-driven scholarly review systems.
